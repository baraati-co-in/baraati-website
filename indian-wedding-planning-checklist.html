Planning guide

The Indian wedding planning checklist.

51 tasks across ten phases, working backwards from your wedding date — from setting the guest-list band twelve months out to confirming the ID documents were deleted afterwards.

Last updated 2 August 2026 · Baraati LLP, Pune

Most Indian wedding checklists cover vendors, budget and venue well, and then stop. This one keeps going into the part that actually breaks during wedding week: the guest list, the RSVP count, the schedule 300 people are trying to follow, and the travel and rooms and ID documents nobody plans for until they are urgent.

Working backwards beats working forwards. Fix the wedding date first, then place every task relative to it. The phases below are expressed as “months out” for exactly that reason — if your wedding is in six months rather than twelve, start at the 6-month phase and compress what is above it, rather than trying to do everything in order.

The timeline
  1. 12 months out — Decide the shape of the wedding
  2. 10–12 months out — Lock the things everyone else competes for
  3. 8–10 months out — Design and dress
  4. 6 months out — Build the real guest list
  5. 4 months out — Invitations and travel
  6. 2–3 months out — Chase, allocate, confirm
  7. 1 month out — Tighten everything
  8. 1 week out — The last mile
  9. The wedding week — Run it, don't rebuild it
  10. After the wedding — Close it out properly

12 months out

Decide the shape of the wedding

  • Agree the guest-list size band with both families — 100, 300 or 800 changes every decision that follows, including the venue you can even consider.
  • Set a total budget and, more importantly, agree who is paying for what. Write it down; memory is not a budgeting tool.
  • Pick a date window using your muhurat, then check it against peak-season pressure — November to February and April to May are when Indian venues and vendors book out first.
  • Decide city versus destination. A destination wedding roughly doubles the logistics load: travel, room blocks and guest IDs all become your problem.
  • List the ceremonies you are actually holding — roka, sagai, tilak, mehendi, haldi, sangeet, the wedding day, reception. Every one needs a date, a venue and a budget line.

10–12 months out

Lock the things everyone else competes for

  • Book the venue or venues. In peak season this is the single hardest constraint; everything else bends around it.
  • Book the photographer and videographer. Good ones are booked a year out and are usually the vendor couples most regret compromising on.
  • Book the caterer, or confirm what the venue's in-house catering includes.
  • If it is a destination wedding, block hotel rooms now and confirm the check-in ID requirements in writing — you will need to collect documents later, and it is far easier to know the format up front.
  • Start a vendor tracker with every contact, quote, advance paid and balance due. A shared dashboard beats a WhatsApp thread you cannot search.

8–10 months out

Design and dress

  • Book the decorator and agree a look for each function — the mehendi, sangeet and wedding usually want three different moods.
  • Begin bridal outfit commissions. A custom lehenga takes four to six months, plus fittings.
  • Book the makeup artist and hair stylist, for the bride and for the family members who want them.
  • Book music — DJ, live band, dhol players, and the sangeet choreographer if there will be performances.
  • Book the priest or officiant and confirm the ritual sequence and timings for each ceremony.

6 months out

Build the real guest list

  • Turn the guest-list band into actual names, with phone numbers and email addresses, in one file both families can edit.
  • Tag every guest by side and group — bride's side, groom's side, college friends, office, the bus from Delhi. You will use these groups constantly later.
  • Mark who is travelling in and who will need a hotel room. That subset is where almost all your logistics effort will go.
  • Decide how you will collect RSVPs. Decide now, because the answer determines the format of your invitation.
  • Plan invitation design — printed card, digital, or both.

4 months out

Invitations and travel

  • Finalise and print physical cards if you are having them; allow three to four weeks for printing.
  • Set up your wedding's guest app and enter every ceremony with its date, time, venue, map link and dress code.
  • Send the invitation and open RSVPs. Sending earlier than this is fine; sending later leaves too little time to chase.
  • Tell out-of-town guests the dates in writing so they can book flights while fares are reasonable.
  • Book transport — airport pickups, shuttles between venues, the groom's car or ghodi.

2–3 months out

Chase, allocate, confirm

  • Chase the guests who have not replied. Do it as a targeted reminder to non-responders, not another broadcast to all 300 people.
  • Give the caterer a first headcount, and agree the deadline for the final one.
  • Allocate hotel rooms against confirmed guests and share each guest's room with them directly.
  • Collect government-ID documents if the hotel, venue or an airline requires them. Do not collect these over WhatsApp — use something that encrypts them, restricts who can see them, and deletes them afterwards.
  • Confirm the trousseau, jewellery and family outfits, with fittings complete.
  • Write the running order for each function and share it with the vendors who need it.

1 month out

Tighten everything

  • Give the caterer and venue the final headcount.
  • Confirm every vendor in writing: arrival time, contact person on the day, and balance due.
  • Build the travel roster — every guest's arrival flight or train, with times — and turn it into a pickup schedule.
  • Assign a point person for each function who is not the couple and not the parents. Someone must be reachable who is not in the mandap.
  • Circulate the full schedule to all guests, with venues, timings and dress codes per function.
  • Confirm the honeymoon bookings and check passport validity and visas.

1 week out

The last mile

  • Reconfirm every vendor and share a one-page contact sheet with all of them.
  • Send guests a final reminder with the schedule, venue maps and dress codes.
  • Distribute welcome kits and room assignments to arriving guests.
  • Brief whoever is handling payments on the day, with envelopes prepared in advance.
  • Pack an emergency kit: safety pins, spare dupatta, phone chargers, painkillers, a sewing kit, and a power bank that actually works.

The wedding week

Run it, don't rebuild it

  • Send a push or message the morning of each function with the time and venue. Assume nobody remembers.
  • Keep one channel for real updates so a change of time is not buried under photographs and congratulations.
  • Delegate photo collection — set the moments you want captured and let guests do the capturing.
  • Eat something. This is the most commonly skipped item on every wedding checklist ever written.

After the wedding

Close it out properly

  • Settle final vendor balances and collect receipts.
  • Collect the photos in one place while people still remember to share them.
  • Send thank-you notes — physical cards still land better than a message, and they are the last impression your wedding leaves.
  • Return rented items, and follow up on the photographer's and videographer's delivery timelines.
  • Confirm that any ID documents you collected have actually been deleted.

What to keep this in

A note on the ceremonies themselves

The checklist above assumes you already know which functions you are holding. If some of the names are unfamiliar — or you are explaining them to guests attending an Indian wedding for the first time — the glossary of Indian wedding terms defines roughly forty of them, including the Sikh, Muslim, Bengali, Gujarati and South Indian variants.
